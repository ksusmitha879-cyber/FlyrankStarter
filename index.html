Which pages to refresh first: scoring content for next-window click decline

karanam Susmitha · Applied Search Intelligence capstone · 01-11-2026

Abstract

Content teams cannot refresh every page, so they need a defensible order for review. We ask which pages are most likely to lose at least 30% of their clicks in the next 28 days. We build features from a trailing click, impression, and position window and label pages by their following window. We compare a logistic regression and a gradient-boosted model against a hand rule that ranks by recent click drop, on one client-held-out, newest-window split.On held-out clients in the newest window, 77 of the logistic model’s top 100 pages declined by at least 30%, against 65 for the baseline rule and a 68.9% base rate across all pages tested.The result supports review prioritization; it does not show that refreshing a page changes its traffic.

Introduction and problem statement

The decision: given a fixed review budget of K pages per cycle, which pages go first? A useful ranking puts pages that will actually decline near the top. The baseline a team would use today is "largest recent drop first," so the model has to beat that on the same pages to be worth running.A content team can review only a small share of its pages each cycle, so the order of review decides where effort goes. A ranking is only worth using if it beats the simple rule the team would use anyway. Here the simple rule did no better than chance in its top 100, so a more careful ranking is worth testing.

Data

Source: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release, fact_content_daily_performance, covering 11 February to 30 June 2026. Page and client identifiers are anonymized; no URLs, titles, queries, or client names are used. We excluded page-windows with fewer than 20 clicks in the trailing window n removed of N, because ratios on near-zero counts are noise. We excluded 111,955 of 870,954 page-windows because the client’s Search Console history did not cover the full feature window, and 733,452 of the remaining 758,999 because they had fewer than 20 clicks in the trailing window, leaving 25,547 page-windows from 30 clients. We did not use the 90-day query table, because its window overlaps the newest label period and would leak the outcome, and we used no GA4 columns..

Methodology

Label. A page-window is positive if clicks in the next window are at least 30% below clicks in the trailing window. This threshold and the window length are choices; section Limitations reports sensitivity to them.

Features. Recent clicks, prior-window clicks, impressions, average position, click momentum, and click-through rate, all computed only from dates on or before the cut. No static features were used; all six features come from Search Console clicks, impressions, and position in the two trailing 28-day windows..

Baseline. Rank by largest relative click drop between the two trailing windows.

Validation. Five rolling origins. Training uses older origins and training clients; testing uses the newest origin and clients never seen in training. Rows are never split randomly, because the same page recurs across origins.

Leakage checks. Label windows begin strictly after the feature window; split is by client and time; each feature was tested alone and none exceeded. none exceeded 0.535 (average position; the range was 0.483 to 0.535). No feature leaks the label, and equally no single feature predicts it well.

The interval only just excludes zero, and it resamples pages rather than clients, so with seven test clients it understates the uncertainty; we treat the advantage as suggestive, not established. Overall ranking quality is close to chance (AUC 0.524 for logistic regression, 0.528 for gradient boosting), and at P@200 the logistic model (0.70) is level with the base rate.

Limitations and honest framing

These are observed associations and a ranking that was measured on past data; they are decision support, not proof. Specifically: (1) the model predicts click decline, not whether a refresh would reverse it, so refresh impact is untested; (2) seasonality can look like decline, and 11 February to 30 June 20263 origins cannot separate the two; (3) results depend on the label threshold and window length — sensitivity at at decline thresholds of 20%, 30%, and 40%, the logistic model’s P@100 was 0.90, 0.77, and 0.66 against baseline values of 0.74, 0.65, and 0.56, ahead at all three, though the window length was not varied from 28 days (4) the test set has 7 held-out clients, so intervals are wide. Nothing here is a claim about how Google's ranking works. Pages were selected for having at least 20 clicks in the trailing window, which favors recent highs and likely raises the share that later decline. Several clients stopped reporting in the final days of the newest label window, which may inflate declines in the test cut

Ranked recommendations

Review pages in score order. Each carries reason codes (the two features contributing most to its score) and an action.

Read this queue as a watch list, not a refresh list. The highest-scoring pages are mostly pages whose clicks recently rose, and the pattern in this analysis suggests they are the ones most likely to fall back. Pages with falling clicks are tagged "refresh review", pages with a recent surge are tagged "monitor: recent surge, may partly revert", and the rest are tagged "monitor". The momentum thresholds of ±0.3 are judgment calls, not tuned.
